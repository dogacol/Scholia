One must learn to see, one must learn to think, one must learn to speak and write: the object in view in all three cases is a noble culture. To learn to see—to accustom the eye to repose, to patience, to letting things come to it; to defer judgment, and to grasp the individual case in all its subtleties.

Learning to think must, of course, be taught in our schools: the concept of “education” has now lost all meaning. The aim should be to train men who are supple, inventive, and deft in their grasp of experience, who know how to discriminate quickly and subtlety. The over-valuation of logic in education is the death of everything fine.

To learn to speak is to learn to give a charm to one’s feelings and thoughts. Speech should be a fine art and not an accidental discharge of raw emotion; it is the means of bridging distances between souls. Who would not scorn the bunglers that they call “orators” to-day?
